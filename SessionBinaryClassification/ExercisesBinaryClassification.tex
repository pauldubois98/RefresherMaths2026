	\subsection{Theory}
	Define a line in $\R^2$ with parameters $\vecw = \begin{pmatrix} w_1 \\ w_2 \end{pmatrix}$ and $b$
	\footnote{
		Here, $\vecw = \begin{pmatrix} w_1 \\ w_2 \end{pmatrix}$, $\vecx = \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix}$, and $b$ is a scalar.
		We use this notation because it makes it very easy to extend the technique to higher dimensions.
		For example, you can just add one component to use a plane to separate points in $\mathbb{R}^3$
	}
	defined by $\vecw.\vecx = b$ (or $\vecw.\vecx - b = 0$) for $\vecx = \begin{pmatrix} x \\ y \end{pmatrix} \in \R^2$.
	This line cut the plane in 2 regions:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item $\mathcal{R}_{-1}$: $\vecx \in \R^2$ such that $\vecw.\vecx - b < 0$
		\item $\mathcal{R}_{+1}$: $\vecx \in \R^2$ such that $\vecw.\vecx - b > 0$
	\end{itemize}
	The goal is to find $\vecw$ and $b$ such that all points of the first class are in the first region, and all points of the second class are in the second region.
	
	Let our data be $\{ (\vecxk, y_k) \}_{k=1}^N$ where $\vecxk$ is the coordinate of the point in $\R^2$ and $y_k$ is the label ($+1$ or $-1$).
	
	The best line is not only separating the data in two sets, but also maximizing the distance between the line and the points.
	
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item Calculate the distance between the line and a point $\vecx \in \R^2$.
		% distance: $\frac{\mid \vecw \cdot \vecx - b \mid}{\mid \mid \vecw \mid \mid}$
	\end{enumerate}
	
	\vspace{0.5cm}
	
	Let $h(\vecx) = \vecw^T \cdot \vecx - b$.
	We will predict first class $-1$ if $h(\vecx)<0$ (as this means $\vecx \in \mathcal{R}_{-1}$); and second class $+1$ if $h(\vecx)>0$ (as this means $\vecx \in \mathcal{R}_{+1}$).
	To have each item of data classified correctly, we need:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item if $y_k = +1$ then $h(\vecxk)>0$
		\item if $y_k = -1$ then $h(\vecxk)<0$
	\end{itemize}
	\begin{enumerate}
		\setcounter{enumi}{1}
		\item Derive a condition that ensures correct classification if true for all $k$.
		% need: $y_k \cdot h(\vecxk) > 0 \ \forall k$.
	\end{enumerate}
	
	\vspace{0.5cm}
	
	Assuming the data is linearly separable, we now define 3 regions:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item $\mathcal{R}_{-1}$: $\vecx \in \R^2$ such that $\vecw.\vecx - b < -1$
		\item $\mathcal{R}_{ 0}$: $\vecx \in \R^2$ such that $\mid \vecw.\vecx - b \mid < 1$ \textit{(the "margin band")}
		\item $\mathcal{R}_{+1}$: $\vecx \in \R^2$ such that $\vecw.\vecx - b > 1$
	\end{itemize}
	\begin{enumerate}
		\setcounter{enumi}{2}
		\item Calculate the margin band width.
		% margin size: $\frac{2}{\mid \mid \vecw \mid \mid}$
		\item Formulate the constrained optimization problem to find the best (i.e. maximizing the margin) parameters $\vecw$ and $b$.
		% problem: $\text{minimize}_{\vecw, b} \mid \mid \vecw \mid \mid$
		% such that $y_k (\vecw.\vecxk - b) \geq 1 \quad \forall k$
	\end{enumerate}
	
	\subsection{Practice}

	Positive class ("+1"):

		\item (1, 1)

	Negative class ("-1"):
	\begin{itemize}

	\begin{enumerate}
		\item Plot the points in a 2D graph.
		\item Using the graph, find the optimal $\vecw$.
		\item Calculate the optimal value of \(b\) to separate the data points while maximizing the margin.
		\item Determine the equation of the optimal hyperplane in the form \(\vecw \cdot \vecx + b = 0\).
		\item Identify the support vectors in the dataset.
		\item Calculate the margin, which is the perpendicular distance from the hyperplane to the nearest support vector.
		\item Classify a new data point, \((3, 2)\), based on the learned SVM model.
	\end{enumerate}
	\textit{Remarks:}\\
	Here, we have made educated guesses to find $\vecw$ and $b$; in practice, we need to solve the constrained optimization problem stated above.\\
	This can be done using Lagrange multiplier, the details are outside the scope of this course.\\
	In practice, we let computers solve the problem for us.

	\section{Logistic Regression}

	The SVM answers with a hard "yes" or "no".
	Often we would rather have a \textbf{probability}: not "this student will pass", but "this student passes with probability $0.8$".
	This is what logistic regression gives us.

	Throughout this section, we work with the following data: the number of hours a student has studied, and whether they passed the exam.

	\begin{table}[h!]
		\centering
		\begin{tabular}{|c|c|}
			\hline
			Hours Studied ($X$) & Pass ($Y$) \\
			\hline
			0 & 0 (no) \\
			4 & 0 (no) \\
			3 & 1 (yes) \\
			8 & 1 (yes) \\
			\hline
		\end{tabular}
	\end{table}

	\noindent
	The model we want to fit is
	$$P(Y=1 \mid X=x) = \sigma\left(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x\right) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(\beta_0 + \beta_1 x)}}$$
	where $\sigma$ is the \textbf{sigmoid} function
	$$\sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1+e^{-z}}$$

	\subsection{The sigmoid function}
	Before fitting anything, let us get familiar with $\sigma$.
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item Compute $\sigma(0)$, $\sigma(2)$ and $\sigma(-2)$.
		\item What are the limits of $\sigma(z)$ as $z \to +\infty$ and as $z \to -\infty$?
		Deduce that $\sigma$ takes its values in $(0,1)$ --- which is what allows us to read it as a probability.
		\item Show that $\sigma(-z) = 1 - \sigma(z)$.\\
		\textit{Hint:} multiply the numerator and the denominator of $\sigma(-z)$ by $e^{-z}$.
		\item Show that
		$$\sigma'(z) = \sigma(z)\left(1 - \sigma(z)\right)$$
		This surprisingly simple derivative is the reason logistic regression is so pleasant to optimize; we will use it in Section 2.4.
		\item Sketch the graph of $\sigma$.
		For which value of $z$ does the model hesitate between the two classes?
	\end{enumerate}

	\subsection{Odds and log-odds}
	Gamblers do not speak of the probability $p$ of an event, but of its \textbf{odds} $\frac{p}{1-p}$ (a probability of $0.75$ means odds of $3$, i.e. "3 to 1").

	\begin{enumerate}
		\item Let $p = \sigma(z)$. Show that
		$$\frac{p}{1-p} = e^{z}
		\qquad \text{and therefore} \qquad
		\log\left(\frac{p}{1-p}\right) = z$$
		\item Deduce that, for our model,
		$$\log\left(\frac{P(Y=1 \mid X=x)}{P(Y=0 \mid X=x)}\right) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x$$
		In words: logistic regression is a \textit{linear} model --- not of the probability, but of the \textbf{log-odds}.
		\item Suppose a student studies one hour more.
		By what factor are their odds of passing multiplied?
		(Your answer should depend on $\beta_1$ only.)
	\end{enumerate}
	\textit{This is how logistic regression is read in practice: the coefficient $\beta_1$ is not a change in probability, it is a multiplier on the odds.}

	\subsection{Measuring how good a model is}
	Take, for now, the (arbitrary) parameters
	$$\beta_0 = -2 \qquad \text{and} \qquad \beta_1 = 0.6$$

	\begin{enumerate}
		\item Compute the four predicted probabilities $p_k = P(Y=1 \mid X = x_k)$ for the four students of the table.
		\item If we predict "pass" whenever $p_k > 0.5$, how many of the four students are correctly classified?
		\item The \textbf{log loss} of a single data point is
		$$\ell(x_k, y_k) = -\left( y_k \log(p_k) + (1-y_k) \log(1-p_k) \right)$$
		Explain why, since $y_k$ is either $0$ or $1$, this is always equal to
		$$-\log\left(\text{the probability the model gave to the \textit{correct} answer}\right)$$
		\item Compute the log loss of each of the four points.
		Which point costs the most? Does that match your intuition?
		\item The \textbf{total cost} is $\mathcal{L}(\beta_0,\beta_1) = \sum_{k=1}^{4} \ell(x_k,y_k)$.
		Compute it.
		\item What would the total cost be for a model that predicts $p = \frac{1}{2}$ for everybody (i.e. $\beta_0 = \beta_1 = 0$)?
		Is our model better than that?
		\item What happens to the log loss of a point if the model gives probability $0$ to the correct answer?
		Why is this a desirable property for a cost function?
	\end{enumerate}

	\subsection{Fitting the model}
	We now look for the $\beta_0$ and $\beta_1$ that make the total cost as small as possible.
	Unlike the linear regression of Session 3, there is no formula for the answer: we must use gradient descent.

	\begin{enumerate}
		\item Using $\sigma' = \sigma(1-\sigma)$, show that for a single data point
		$$\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \beta_0} = p_k - y_k
		\qquad \text{and} \qquad
		\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial \beta_1} = x_k \left( p_k - y_k \right)$$
		and deduce the two partial derivatives of the total cost $\mathcal{L}$.\\
		\textit{Remark:} the quantity $p_k - y_k$ is simply the error made on the $k$-th point. It is hard to imagine a simpler gradient.
		\item Write the two update rules of gradient descent for $\beta_0$ and $\beta_1$, with a learning rate $\eta$.
		\item Starting from $\beta_0 = \beta_1 = 0$ and with $\eta = 0.05$, perform \textbf{one} step by hand.
		Comment on the sign of the change of $\beta_1$.
		\item Using a computer, iterate until convergence and give the fitted $\beta_0$ and $\beta_1$, and the minimal cost.
		(A notebook is provided; you may also use the grid search of Session 3 to get a first rough answer.)
		\item At the minimum, the gradient vanishes.
		Show that this means
		$$\sum_{k} p_k = \sum_{k} y_k
		\qquad \text{and} \qquad
		\sum_{k} x_k \, p_k = \sum_{k} x_k \, y_k$$
		and use these two identities to check your numerical answer.
	\end{enumerate}

	\subsection{Reading the fitted model}
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item Estimate the probability of passing for a student who studied $0$, $1$ and $10$ hours.
		\item Above how many hours of study does the model predict a pass?
		(This is the \textbf{decision boundary}; express it in terms of $\beta_0$ and $\beta_1$ before computing it.)
		\item Using Section 2.2, give the factor by which one extra hour of study multiplies the odds of passing.
		\item Can this model classify all four students of the table correctly?
		Look again at the data before answering, and explain what this implies for the minimal cost you found.
		\item Would you trust these numbers to advise a real student?
	\end{enumerate}
